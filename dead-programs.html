Grant programs that look open but aren't

Listing sites rarely revisit a page once it is published. Here are four programs still described as accepting applications that are not, why this keeps happening, and how to check in two minutes.

In this guide
  • Why this keeps happening
  • Four verified examples
  • The version that is harder to catch
  • How to check in two minutes

Why this keeps happening

Nobody is lying to you. What happens is more mundane and, in a way, more annoying: a grant listing site publishes a page describing a program, and then never looks at it again. The program is suspended, or the administering organization merges into another agency, or the sponsor quietly declines to fund another round. None of those events generate a press release. The listing stays up, ranking in search, for years.

The cost lands entirely on the business owner. You find a program that fits, spend a week assembling documents, and discover at the point of submission that the portal is closed, or that the organization no longer exists under that name. That is a week you could have spent on a program that was actually open.

The underlying problem: a listing is a snapshot. A program is a thing that changes. Any source that publishes once and does not re-check is guaranteed to be wrong eventually, and it will not tell you when it becomes wrong.

Four verified examples

All four were checked against the administrator's own materials in July 2026. All four were, at that time, still described as open somewhere on the internet.

Etsy Emergency Relief Fund

A $2,000 grant for Etsy sellers affected by federally declared disasters, administered by CERF+. CERF+ announced on its own website that after evaluating its financial position, it has suspended all grantmaking programs for the remainder of its 2026 fiscal year. Aggregator listings updated as recently as June 2026 still described the fund as open and accepting applications.

DoorDash Restaurant Disaster Relief Fund

Grants of $5,000 to $15,000 for independently owned businesses hit by declared disasters, run with Hello Alice and the Global Entrepreneurship Network. The Hello Alice portal that actually receives applications states the fund is not currently accepting them, and that rounds open around specific declared disasters. Listings from 2026 described it as open, some claiming quarterly rounds you could apply to immediately.

Empower Digital Grant

A Massachusetts matching grant for technology and digital marketing, run by the Massachusetts Growth Capital Corporation. MGCC has been absorbed into MassDevelopment, and the program's own application link now redirects to MassDevelopment's homepage. Grant sites still list it as active in 2026, and the old link still circulates.

A workforce grant that no longer exists

This one is the clearest illustration. A regional business databank lists a Massachusetts workforce program offering a hiring incentive grant of $30,000 a year. The state agency that runs the workforce fund no longer offers that program at all; its current published list contains four grant types and that is not among them. The same listing also states a maximum award roughly five times the real current cap for the program it does still describe correctly.

We only caught that one because we went looking for the source. It reads as authoritative: a real regional organization, specific dollar figures, plausible program names. Specificity is not accuracy.

The version that is harder to catch

A closed program is at least honest about itself once you reach the application page. The harder problem is a program that is genuinely open but described with stale numbers: last cycle's award ceiling, a match requirement that has since changed, an eligibility rule that was relaxed two years ago.

This is worse because nothing fails. You apply, you are eligible, and you have simply planned around the wrong figures. We have seen a listing overstate a program's maximum award by a factor of five. A business budgeting against that number builds a project it cannot fund.

Rule of thumb: a dollar figure, a match percentage or a deadline from anywhere other than the administering agency should be treated as a starting point for verification, never as a planning input.

How to check in two minutes

Before you invest real time in an application, run these. They catch the large majority of stale listings.

  • Go to the administrator, not the listing. Find the organization that actually receives applications and read their page. For corporate programs this is usually a nonprofit intermediary, not the company on the logo.
  • Look for a year. A live program names its current cycle. A page that says "applications are now open" with no year attached has probably said that for a long time.
  • Check the organization still exists under that name. Agency mergers are common and they orphan program pages. If the link redirects to a homepage, that is your answer.
  • Find the most recent recipients. Real programs publish winners. If the latest cohort is three years old, treat the program as dormant until proven otherwise.
  • Verify the numbers at the source. Award ceilings and match requirements change more often than people expect, and stale figures are the failure mode that does not announce itself.

None of this is difficult. It is just tedious, and it has to be repeated every time, for every program, forever. That is precisely the kind of work that gets skipped when you are also running a business.
